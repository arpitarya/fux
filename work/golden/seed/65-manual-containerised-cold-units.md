# Containerised Unit Operating Manual

How the engineering pool's containerised cold rooms are placed, powered,
started, used, moved and looked after. The units are hired out to customers who
need cold space where Quillfern has no building: hospitals running vaccination
campaigns, a dairy cooperative's collection centre in the flush season, a
programme that needs a small store in a city neighbourhood. The manual is for
the engineers who place and service the units and for the customer's people who
use them day to day.

## 1. The six units

1.1. The pool owns six units, MCR-1 to MCR-6. Each is a 20-foot insulated
container with a refrigeration unit at one end, a personnel door at the other,
internal shelving and a controller that logs the inside temperature every
minute.

1.2. MCR-1 to MCR-4 run at +2 to +8 C and are used for chilled medicine and
chilled food. MCR-5 and MCR-6 can also run at -20 C and are used for frozen
stock or for vaccines that are shipped frozen.

1.3. At any time about four units are out on campaign hires of a few weeks and
two are on long hires. The pool coordinator keeps the deployment board.

1.4. Every unit carries its number on both long sides and on the door. A unit is
never relabelled, even after a refurbishment.

1.5. The units were bought in 2024 and 2025. Their refrigeration units are under
the maker's warranty until the dates written on the inside of each door.

## 2. Choosing the ground

2.1. A unit stands on firm, level ground, with a gradient no steeper than 1 in
100. Concrete or compacted hardcore is acceptable; soil, sand and grass are not.

2.2. The fan side of the refrigeration unit must breathe. Leave one and a half metres free on the fan side, and keep that strip clear of vehicles, stacked crates and tarpaulins. A condenser that recirculates its own warm air loses
capacity on the first hot afternoon.

2.3. The unit is placed out of standing water. A site that floods in the
monsoon is refused, or the unit is raised on the pool's steel stands.

2.4. The personnel door needs room to open fully and a step that the customer's
staff can use safely while carrying cartons.

2.5. The site must be lit at night and must be inside the customer's boundary,
behind a gate that is locked or guarded.

2.6. The engineer who places the unit photographs the site from four sides and
files the photographs with the placement sheet.

## 3. Power

3.1. A unit needs a three-phase supply of 415 V, 32 A, through the pool's own
weatherproof socket, which the customer's electrician fits before the unit
arrives.

3.2. Every campaign hire also has a standby genset of the pool's own, wired
through an automatic transfer switch. The transfer switch must bring the standby set on line within 30 seconds of mains loss. The changeover is tested
at commissioning by pulling the mains isolator.

3.3. Long hires at sites with a reliable supply and their own standby may use
the site's standby instead of the pool's, if the pool engineer has tested the
changeover and written the result on the placement sheet.

3.4. The genset's fuel is the customer's responsibility on a campaign hire. The
pool engineer shows the customer's staff how to read the gauge and how to call
for fuel before the tank is low.

3.5. Extension leads, adaptors and shared sockets are never used.

## 4. First cooling at a new site

4.1. A unit arriving at a new site is cleaned inside, powered and closed with
nothing in it. The controller is set to the hire's set point.

4.2. The unit must pull down to its set point and then hold its range, empty,
for the stability hold before any stock goes in. The current hold is 24 hours.

4.3. Revision 2 of February 2026 doubled the hold from 12 to 24 hours after the Satara campaign unit drifted on its second night. The drift came from a
controller probe that had been knocked out of its clip in transit; it showed
only after the first night's cold soak.

4.4. During the hold the engineer checks the controller log at least twice,
including once after dark.

4.5. The customer is told on booking that the unit cannot take stock on its
first day on site. A customer who needs stock in on day one is offered an
earlier placement date, not a shorter hold.

4.6. At the end of the hold the engineer signs the placement sheet and hands
the customer's lead the key and the duty phone number.

## 5. Getting in and out

5.1. The personnel door is the only way into a unit. It is kept shut except
while someone is passing through it with stock.

5.2. The door has a strip curtain behind it and a plastic step outside. Staff
take what they need, close the door, and then sort or count outside, not in the
doorway.

5.3. The door switch is wired to the controller. The controller sounds after 90 seconds ajar and calls the duty phone after three minutes.

5.4. The customer's staff cannot silence the door sounder; only closing the
door does. A door that will not close is reported to the duty phone at once.

5.5. Nobody stays inside a unit with the door shut. The inside handle opens the
door from inside even when it is padlocked outside, and this is checked at every
service.

## 6. Defrost and the drain

6.1. Chilled units defrost four times a day for about 20 minutes each time.
Frozen units defrost every six hours. The controller log shows each defrost.

6.2. Defrost water runs out through a heated drain at the condenser end. The
drain outlet is kept clear of soil, leaves and plastic.

6.3. A short rise in air temperature during defrost is expected and is not a
fault. Stock temperature hardly moves.

6.4. A unit that ices up inside is reported to the duty phone. The customer's
staff do not chip ice from the evaporator.

## 7. What a customer pays

7.1. A unit is hired by the day. Charging is Rs. 4,800 a day, and no booking is billed for fewer than 14 days, however early the unit is released.

7.2. A long hire, meaning ninety days or more, is Rs. 3,900 a day.

7.3. Delivery and collection are charged separately at the pool's transport
rate, and the standby genset on a campaign hire is included in the daily
charge.

7.4. The pool coordinator raises the hire invoice monthly in arrears. Fuel for
the genset is the customer's own cost.

7.5. A unit damaged by the customer's people is repaired at the customer's
cost, against the engineer's report and photographs.

7.6. The daily charge stops on the day the unit leaves the customer's site,
not on the day the customer stops using it.

## 8. Moving a unit

8.1. Units are moved by the pool's crane lorry and its trained crew, never by
the customer's own equipment.

8.2. Nothing is ever lifted with product inside it; the unit is emptied into a reefer first, even for a move of fifty metres.

8.3. A move within a site is treated as a new placement: the ground is checked,
the power is checked, and the unit holds range empty before stock goes back in.

8.4. The controller is switched off for the lift and back on as soon as the
unit is set down.

8.5. The crew lead decides whether a lift is safe. Overhead lines, soft ground
or a crowd in the lifting area stop the lift.

## 9. The engineer's calendar

9.1. Every unit has a compressor hour meter. Every 1,000 hours on the compressor meter, or every four months if that comes first, the unit gets a full visit.

9.2. A full visit covers the refrigerant charge, the condenser coil, the fans,
the door seal, the heaters, the drain, the controller probe and the alarm call.

9.3. The engineer writes the meter reading and the work done on the unit's
service card, which hangs inside the door, and in the pool's own log.

9.4. A unit that misses a visit is taken off hire at the end of its current
booking until the visit is done.

9.5. Controller probes are checked against a calibrated reference at every full
visit.

## 10. Alarms and the duty phone

10.1. The controller calls the duty phone for a high or low temperature, a door
left open, a power failure and a controller fault.

10.2. The pool engineer on duty answers the duty phone at all hours and calls
back the customer's lead within 15 minutes.

10.3. A temperature alarm is handled under the company's excursion rules. The
customer's staff do not move stock out of the unit unless the engineer tells
them to.

10.4. Each alarm and its outcome are written in the pool's alarm log.

## 11. Cleaning

11.1. The customer's staff wipe the shelves and floor weekly with the cleaner
supplied by the pool. Water is never sprayed inside a unit.

11.2. A unit is deep-cleaned by the pool between every two hires and whenever a
spill has been reported.

11.3. Food and medicine are never in the same unit during one hire.

## 12. The customer's people

12.1. The customer names a lead for each unit. The lead is shown the door, the
controller display, the duty phone and the genset gauge at placement.

12.2. The lead keeps a simple sheet of who went in and when, and the sheet is
collected at each service visit.

12.3. Nobody under 18 goes into a unit.

## 13. Records

13.1. The placement sheet, the service cards, the alarm log and the controller
logs are kept for three years, or seven where a temperature alarm was raised
during a pharma hire.

13.2. Controller logs are downloaded at every service visit and at the end of
every hire.

## 14. Changes to this manual

14.1. Revision 2 (February 2026) lengthened the stability hold after a campaign
unit drifted.

14.2. Revision 3 (July 2026) added the long-hire daily charge and the rule that
a move within a site is a new placement.

14.3. The pool lead reviews this manual every January.
