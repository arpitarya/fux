# Product Testing Laboratory Manual

## 1. Purpose

1.1. The Product Testing Laboratory is Quillfern's own small laboratory, on the mezzanine of the Bhosari hub. It opened in October 2025 to answer questions that the company used to send to outside laboratories at a cost of days and money: whether a cleaned surface is clean, whether a returned food pack is still sound, whether a new packaging design keeps a meal kit cold on a doorstep, and whether a component from a new supplier is as strong as its certificate says.

1.2. This manual says what the laboratory does, how samples reach it, how tests are run and reported, how the laboratory proves that its results can be trusted, and how it keeps its people safe. It is written for the laboratory's analysts, for the QA officers and engineers who send it samples, and for customers' auditors who want to know how a result was reached.

1.3. The laboratory is not accredited for regulatory testing. Results it reports are for Quillfern's own decisions and for customers who have agreed in writing to rely on them. Where a law or a customer requires an accredited result, the sample goes to an accredited outside laboratory, and this laboratory only prepares and ships it.

1.4. Revision 2 changes several test conditions and checking frequencies after the laboratory's first six months, and adds the section on comparison with other laboratories. Each changed section says what it replaced.

## 2. The laboratory and its people

2.1. The laboratory occupies about 140 square metres: a sample reception counter, a microbiology room with its own air handling, a chemistry and physical testing room, an environmental chamber for packaging tests, a drop-test rig in a bay on the hub floor, a washing room and a small office.

2.2. The laboratory head, Dr. Kirti Bapat, is a microbiologist who spent nine years in a dairy company's quality laboratory. Omkar Thorat leads the physical and packaging tests. Two analysts work in the laboratory on the day shift, Monday to Saturday.

2.3. The laboratory head reports to the head of QA, not to the hub manager, so that nobody who must meet a dispatch plan can lean on a result.

2.4. Analysts are trained on each test method by the laboratory head and authorised in writing before they run it alone. The list of authorised analysts is posted inside the laboratory door and kept current.

2.5. Visitors to the laboratory, including customers and auditors, are escorted and wear laboratory coats. They do not enter the microbiology room while plates are open.

## 3. Asking for a test

3.1. Tests are requested on the laboratory request form, from the QA system or on paper. The form names the person asking, the reason for the test, the sample, the tests wanted and the date the result is needed.

3.2. The laboratory head decides whether the laboratory can do the test, whether it should go to an outside laboratory, and when the result will be ready. Requests marked urgent are discussed by phone the same day.

3.3. The laboratory does not test anything without knowing why. A request that says only "please test" is returned with a question.

3.4. Requests from customers come through their account managers and are confirmed by the head of QA before the laboratory accepts them.

## 4. Samples arriving

4.1. Samples are received at the reception counter by an analyst, who checks them against the request form, labels each one with a laboratory number and records the condition and temperature on arrival.

4.2. Chilled samples arrive in a cooled box with a temperature indicator; frozen samples arrive in a frozen box. A sample that arrives warm, leaking or unlabelled is recorded as received in that condition and the requester is asked whether to test it anyway.

4.3. Samples are stored at their proper temperature in the laboratory's own fridges and freezers, which are separate from the hub's stores and carry their own loggers.

4.4. Food and packaging samples from customers belong to the customer. Nothing is used for anything other than the test requested, and nothing is eaten, taken home or given away.

## 5. Growing what is there

5.1. The laboratory counts bacteria, yeasts and moulds on food samples and on surface swabs, so that QA can decide whether a product, a liner or a body is fit to use.

5.2. For the total viable count, the prepared sample is spread on plate count agar and the plates are incubated at 30 C for 72 hours before they are read. Coliforms, yeasts and moulds follow their own methods, listed in the laboratory's method file.

5.3. Until January 2026 the count was read after 48 hours at a higher temperature, which missed slow growers from chilled foods.

5.4. Each incubator is checked twice a day with a reference thermometer, and its reading written on the sheet on its door. One that has drifted more than 1 C from its set point is taken out of use until it has been serviced, and plates inside it are moved to the spare incubator.

5.5. Every batch of agar is tested with a known organism before it is used for samples.

## 6. Quick checks on cleanliness

6.1. Not every cleanliness check can wait three days for a plate count. For surfaces that have just been cleaned, such as the inside of a returned meal-kit liner or a reefer floor before loading, the laboratory uses a rapid swab that gives a reading in seconds.

6.2. The swab detects atp, the energy molecule present in food residue and living cells, and the hand-held reader reports it in rlu. A reading of 100 or below is a pass. From 101 to 300 the surface is cleaned again and swabbed again; above 300 it fails, and the cleaning itself is reviewed.

6.3. Until March 2026 the laboratory's pass line was higher, and liners that passed were later found carrying mould.

6.4. A swab reading measures cleanliness, not safety. A surface that passes still goes through any microbiological test that QA has asked for.

6.5. The atp reader is checked each morning with the maker's control swabs, which give a known rlu figure. A reader that fails its check is not used that day.

## 7. Setting some aside

7.1. A result can be challenged weeks after it was reported, by a customer, an insurer or a supplier who disagrees with it, and the only fair answer to a challenge is retesting the same material.

7.2. Before testing begins, the analyst divides every food sample and sets aside a portion, sealed and labelled with the laboratory number. Each retained aliquot is stored frozen at -20 C for 90 days after the report is issued, and then destroyed unless someone has asked for it to be kept.

7.3. The first revision of this manual kept portions for 30 days, and two challenges arrived after they had gone, leaving nothing for retesting.

7.4. A request to keep an aliquot longer is made to the laboratory head in writing, with the reason, and is listed in the freezer register.

7.5. Packaging samples are not divided; the tested sample itself is kept, retained in the same way for 90 days, on the shelf in the store room.

## 8. Checking ourselves against others

8.1. A laboratory can be consistently wrong without knowing it. The only way to find out is to test samples whose true answer is known to someone else.

8.2. The laboratory takes part in an external proficiency scheme for food microbiology twice a year, in which the scheme sends the same samples to many laboratories and compares their results. A result is satisfactory when its z-score lies between -2 and +2.

8.3. A score between 2 and 3 either way is investigated by the laboratory head and the cause written down. A score beyond 3 stops the laboratory reporting that test to anyone until the cause has been found and a repeat sample has passed.

8.4. For packaging tests, where no scheme exists, the laboratory runs one interlaboratory comparison a year with the box maker's own laboratory, testing samples cut from the same sheets. The two results must agree within the tolerance in the method file.

8.5. Proficiency results and each interlaboratory report are shown to the head of QA and to any customer auditor who asks.

## 9. Measuring small volumes

9.1. Microbiology depends on measuring small volumes of sample and diluent accurately. An error of a few per cent in a single pipette stroke becomes a large error in the count.

9.2. Every pipette in use is checked every three months by weighing the water it delivers on the laboratory balance, at its nominal volume and at a tenth of it, ten times each. The mean must be within 1 per cent of the nominal volume, and the spread within the maker's figure.

9.3. One that fails is labelled out of use and sent to the maker's service agent; one dropped on the floor is checked before it is used again, whatever it looks like.

9.4. The gravimetric check uses the laboratory balance, which is itself checked every morning with a certified weight and calibrated every year by an outside laboratory.

9.5. Results of every check are kept in the equipment file, and gravimetric figures are plotted so that drift shows.

## 10. Physical and packaging tests

10.1. The laboratory tests packaging components and complete kits for customers' programmes: the compression strength of outer shippers, the thickness and density of insulating liners, the seal strength of food packs, and the performance of complete kits in drop tests and in the environmental chamber.

10.2. Every component is conditioned before testing at a fixed temperature and humidity for at least 24 hours, so that results from a wet week and a dry week can be compared.

10.3. The compression tester and the drop rig are checked against reference loads and heights every month, and calibrated by an outside firm every year.

10.4. Test methods for each programme's components are agreed with the programme's packaging engineer and the customer, and filed in the method file. The laboratory does not change a method on its own judgement.

10.5. Results for complete kits are written up as a short report with photographs of each kit after testing, and signed by the analyst and by Omkar Thorat.

## 11. Making things sterile

11.1. Used cultures, plates and swabs are made safe before they leave the laboratory. Glassware and media are sterilised before use.

11.2. Both jobs are done in the autoclave in the washing room. A standard load runs at 121 C for 20 minutes once the whole load has reached that temperature, and every cycle's printout is kept.

11.3. Every week a biological indicator, a spore strip carrying a known number of heat-resistant bacteria, is placed in the middle of a full load. After the cycle the strip is incubated; any growth means the cycle failed, the week's loads are repeated, and the machine is serviced before it is used again.

11.4. Until February 2026 the spore test was done monthly, which would have let three weeks of unsafe waste leave the building after a failure.

11.5. Only trained analysts load and unload the autoclave, wearing heat-resistant gloves and a face shield, and nobody opens the door before the pressure gauge reads zero.

## 12. Reporting results

12.1. Every result is checked by a second analyst against the raw data before it is reported. The checker signs the worksheet.

12.2. Reports go to the person who asked for the test, with a copy to the QA officer for the site concerned. They show the laboratory number, the sample, the method, the result, the date of testing and the name of the analyst.

12.3. A result outside the limit the requester gave is phoned through on the day, before the written report, and the call is recorded.

12.4. Reports state what was tested and what was found. They do not say whether stock should be released or destroyed; that decision belongs to QA.

12.5. A report that has to be corrected is reissued with a new revision number and a note of what changed. The old report is withdrawn, not deleted.

## 13. Safety in the laboratory

13.1. The laboratory handles ordinary food bacteria, not dangerous organisms. Even so, nobody eats, drinks or applies make-up in the laboratory, and coats, gloves and safety glasses are worn at the bench.

13.2. Spills of cultures are covered with disinfectant, left for the time on the disinfectant's label and then cleaned up. The spill is written in the laboratory incident book.

13.3. Chemicals are stored in the chemicals cabinet with their safety data sheets, and incompatible chemicals are kept apart.

13.4. Waste leaves the laboratory only after it has been made safe, through the hub's licensed waste contractor.

13.5. The laboratory has its own eyewash station, first-aid box and fire extinguisher, and every analyst is shown where they are on the first day.

## 14. Records

14.1. Request forms, worksheets, raw data, instrument printouts, reports and equipment records are kept for six years, and longer where a claim or a customer's contract needs them.

14.2. Records are made at the time, in ink or in the laboratory system, and corrected only by striking through and initialling.

14.3. Customers' auditors may see the records of tests on their own samples. They do not see other customers' records.

## 15. Costs and charges

15.1. Tests requested by Quillfern's own departments are charged to the requesting department at the rates in the laboratory's price list, reviewed every April.

15.2. Tests for customers are charged through the customer's account at the rates in their contract, or at the price list where the contract says nothing.

15.3. Samples sent to outside laboratories are charged at the outside laboratory's price plus the cost of shipping.

## 16. Review of this manual

16.1. The laboratory head reviews this manual every June and whenever a method, a piece of equipment or a customer requirement changes.

16.2. Each revision is explained to the analysts at a briefing, and each analyst signs that they have read it.

16.3. Suggestions for changes come from analysts, from the people who send samples and from customers' auditors. Each suggestion is answered in writing by the laboratory head within a month, whether or not it is taken up, and the answers are kept with the manual's revision file.

## 17. Working with outside laboratories

17.1. Some tests are beyond the laboratory's equipment or its authority: pathogen confirmation, chemical residues, allergen testing and any test a regulator or a customer requires from an accredited laboratory. Those samples go to outside laboratories on the approved list kept by QA.

17.2. The laboratory prepares, packs and ships samples for outside testing under the outside laboratory's instructions, with a temperature indicator in every chilled or frozen box. The courier's receipt and the outside laboratory's acknowledgement are filed with the request.

17.3. Results from outside laboratories are sent to the requester by the laboratory head, with a short note if the result needs explaining. The outside report itself is attached unchanged.

17.4. Outside laboratories are reviewed every year on turnaround, on the quality of their reports and on any results that were later shown to be wrong. A laboratory that loses or spoils two samples in a year is removed from the list.

## 18. Training and competence

18.1. Analysts are trained on each method by the laboratory head and authorised in writing only after they have run the method on samples of known result and reached the expected answer three times.

18.2. Each analyst's competence is checked again every year on every method they are authorised for, using blind samples prepared by the laboratory head. An analyst who fails a check is suspended from that method until retrained and checked again.

18.3. Analysts attend at least two external training days a year, chosen with the laboratory head. Notes from each day are shared with the rest of the team at the next weekly meeting.

18.4. The laboratory keeps a training file for each analyst showing every sign-off, every annual check and every course attended.

## 19. Housekeeping

19.1. Benches are cleared and disinfected at the end of each day and before any new piece of work begins. Nothing is left on a bench overnight except work in progress, labelled with its laboratory number and the analyst's initials.

19.2. Fridges and freezers are defrosted and cleaned on a calendar kept on their doors. Out-of-date media, reagents and samples are thrown away at each cleaning, through the waste route for their kind.

19.3. The laboratory floor is cleaned by laboratory staff, not by the hub's cleaning contractor, so that nobody unfamiliar with the work cleans around open cultures.

19.4. A weekly walk-round by the laboratory head checks the state of the rooms, the equipment and the records, and the findings are written in the housekeeping log.

## 20. Customers' samples and confidentiality

20.1. Several customers send the laboratory samples of their own products or packaging, and some of them compete with each other. A customer's samples, results and methods are seen only by the laboratory staff, the head of QA and the customer's own account manager.

20.2. Reports to a customer carry only that customer's results. Comparisons between customers' products are never made, even in conversation, and the laboratory does not tell one customer what another has tested.

20.3. A customer's sample left over after its retention period is destroyed, not kept as a reference for another customer's work. Reference materials the laboratory needs are bought or made for the purpose.

20.4. Photographs taken in the laboratory are of samples and equipment only, and are stored in the laboratory system against the laboratory number. They are not posted in any group chat or sent from personal phones.

20.5. A customer who wants its records removed at the end of its contract asks through its account manager. Records are removed only where no claim, dispute or legal requirement needs them, and the removal is itself recorded.
