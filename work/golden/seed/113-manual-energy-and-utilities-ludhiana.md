# Energy and Utilities Manual, Ludhiana DC

## 1. Purpose and reach

1.1. Ludhiana DC is the first Quillfern building north of the Vindhyas built from the ground up for cold storage, and the first one that pays for its own electricity at an industrial tariff from the day it opened. It stands on the Ladhowal bypass, on a plot the company owns outright, with a chilled block, a frozen block, a small ambient store and a two-storey office facing the gate.

1.2. This manual says how the building gets its power and water, how those supplies are protected, how they are measured, and who answers when one of them fails. It is written for the engineering team on site, for the shift leads who are the first to notice when something is wrong, and for Finance, which pays the bills and wants to know why they move.

1.3. The manual sets no product temperature and changes no quality rule. When a utility fails and stock is at risk, the stock is handled under the company procedures for excursions and quarantine, and this manual only says how to get the plant running again.

1.4. Revision 2 replaces the utilities note written while the building was being commissioned in 2025. That note was useful at the time but several of its figures were guesses made before the site had run through a Punjab summer and a Punjab winter. Where a figure changed, the section that carries it says so.

1.5. The engineering lead, Gurmeet Kaur Sidhu, owns this manual. Harpreet Bains, the electrical supervisor, owns the substation, the switch room and the earthing. The two of them sign every change to the plant that this manual covers, and neither signs alone.

1.6. Copies are kept in the engineering office, in the switch room and at the gate cabin. A copy found anywhere else, including a photograph on a personal phone, is out of date by assumption.

## 2. How power reaches the site

2.1. The site takes its supply at 11,000 volts from a dedicated feeder that leaves the utility's grid substation at Ladhowal, about four kilometres away. The feeder is overhead for most of its length and underground for the last 300 metres, entering the plot at the north-west corner.

2.2. The incoming supply meets the site in the substation yard beside the gate, behind a chain-link fence with a locked gate and a danger board in Punjabi, Hindi and English. The yard holds the utility's metering cubicle, the site's own high-tension breaker panel and the step-down equipment that feeds the main low-tension switchboard.

2.3. From the switchboard the supply divides into five feeders: the refrigeration plant, the chilled block, the frozen block, the office and canteen, and the yard lighting with the gate. Each feeder has its own breaker, its own energy meter and its own label, and the labels are checked against the single-line drawing every January.

2.4. The single-line drawing hangs in the switch room under glass. A change to the electrical system is not complete until the drawing on the wall has been redrawn and countersigned. A pencil correction on the glass is not a change; it is a hazard.

2.5. The utility's linesman may enter the yard to read or test the meter, but he is met at the gate by the electrical supervisor or, at night, by the shift lead with the duty electrician on the phone. Nobody from the utility switches anything inside the plot without the supervisor being present.

2.6. Supply interruptions are frequent in the paddy season, when the rural feeders around Ladhowal are overloaded by tube-well pumps. Most last a few minutes; some last hours. The generators described in section 4 are sized for the long ones.

## 3. Behind the chain-link fence

3.1. The 2 MVA transformer in the substation yard is oil-filled, and its oil is usually the first thing to fail without anyone noticing. Moisture and sludge pull down the oil's dielectric strength long before anything trips.

3.2. A sample is drawn by the approved testing laboratory every six months, in March and September, with the unit on load. The breakdown voltage must read at least 50 kV. A sample below that is filtered on site within seven days and drawn again after filtering.

3.3. The 2025 commissioning note allowed a yearly sample and a lower pass figure; both were tightened after the first monsoon, when condensation was found in the breather.

3.4. The laboratory's report is filed in the substation folder and copied to the engineering lead together with the dielectric figure from each of the last four samples, so that a slow decline is visible before it becomes a failure.

3.5. Nobody opens the transformer enclosure except the electrical supervisor or the utility's linesman, and never alone.

## 4. Generators and the changeover

4.1. Two 1,010 kVA diesel generator sets stand on a plinth outside the plant room, under a canopy, with their own day tanks and a shared bulk tank of 20,000 litres. Either set alone carries the refrigeration plant, the frozen block and the controls. Both together carry the whole site, including the office and the yard lights.

4.2. The changeover is automatic. When the grid supply fails, both sets start, the first to reach speed takes the essential load, and the second joins it if the load needs it. When the grid returns and has been steady for five minutes, the load goes back to the grid and the sets run on unloaded for ten minutes before stopping.

4.3. The changeover is tested under load on the first Wednesday of each month by opening the incoming breaker. The electrical supervisor records the time taken, the frequency and voltage on the panel, and any alarm. A test that fails is repeated the same day after the fault is found.

4.4. The bulk tank is dipped every Monday and after every outage longer than four hours. The tank is never allowed below 8,000 litres between October and March, when fog and frost bring the longest outages, and never below 5,000 litres in the other months. A top-up is ordered from the listed supplier as soon as the dip reads below the figure for the month plus 3,000 litres.

4.5. The generator sets are serviced by the maker's dealer every 250 running hours or every six months, whichever comes first. The service sheet goes into the generator file, and the dealer's engineer signs the generator log beside the hours reading.

4.6. The room holding Vetrix Animal Health's frozen breeding stock has its extractor fan on the essential load, so that it keeps running through any outage. Nobody refuels a running set. Diesel spills are covered with sand from the bins beside the plinth and reported to the shift lead before the end of the shift.

4.7. The sets are loud. The plinth is fenced, and nobody enters the fence while a set is running except the electrical supervisor or the dealer's engineer with ear defenders on.

## 5. What the bill penalises

5.1. The utility bills the site on kilovolt-amperes as well as kilowatt-hours, so a poor power factor costs money every month even when nothing is wrong with the plant. Large compressor motors pull the factor down; the capacitor bank in the switch room pushes it back up.

5.2. The bank is rated at 360 kvar in six automatic steps. The site's target is a monthly average of 0.98. The utility adds a surcharge to any month that averages below 0.90, and gives a small rebate for any month above 0.95.

5.3. When the bank was first installed it had four steps and a lower rating, and the target was 0.95; the extra steps were added in April 2026 after two summer bills carried the surcharge.

5.4. The controller's display, which shows the kvar switched in on each step, is read at every monthly energy review. A step that will not switch in is reported to the electrical supervisor the same day.

5.5. Capacitor cans are never touched until the bank has been isolated and left for the discharge time printed on its door.

## 6. Riding through the gap

6.1. The refrigeration controllers, the alarm panel, the gate barrier and the two servers in the comms room do not wait for a generator. They run through the ups in the comms room, which carries them through the seconds of a changeover and through a generator that fails to start.

6.2. The unit must give at least 45 minutes at full connected load on battery alone. That figure was 20 minutes in the commissioning note, and was raised after a winter night in January 2026 when both sets were slow to start in the fog and the alarm panel went dark for four minutes.

6.3. Autonomy falls as batteries age, so the battery string is replaced every four years, whatever its test results, and load-tested every six months with the site on grid power.

6.4. The autonomy measured at each load test is written in the switch room log beside the date. A string that gives less than the required figure on two tests in a row is replaced early.

6.5. Nothing new is plugged into the ups without the electrical supervisor's written approval. Kettles, heaters and phone chargers found on its outlets are removed.

## 7. Metering and the monthly energy review

7.1. Every feeder carries its own energy meter, read automatically every fifteen minutes into the building management system and printed as a daily sheet that the morning shift lead initials.

7.2. On the fifth working day of each month the engineering lead holds the energy review with the electrical supervisor, the site manager and someone from Finance. The review looks at units consumed by each feeder, the maximum demand recorded by the utility meter, diesel used, water drawn and the cost of each.

7.3. The site's own meter readings are compared with the utility's bill before the bill is paid. A difference of more than one per cent between the two is raised with the utility in writing, with the site's readings attached, and the bill is paid under protest if the due date arrives before the answer.

7.4. Consumption is expressed per pallet position occupied, not per square metre, because a half-empty building that costs the same to run is a commercial problem, not an engineering one. The figure is tracked month by month on a chart in the engineering office.

7.5. The review ends with a short list of actions, each with an owner and a date. Actions carried over from the previous month are read out first.

7.6. Finance receives the review note within three working days. It is the only document Finance uses to explain the electricity line in the site's monthly accounts.

## 8. Sunlight on the chilled block

8.1. The roof of the chilled block carries a grid-tied solar plant of 480 kWp, feeding the site's own load through the main switchboard. It cuts the daytime bill; nothing in the site's plans counts on it during an outage.

8.2. Dust from the ring road and smoke from stubble fires settle on the glass within days, and soiling is the plant's largest avoidable loss. It is estimated each week by comparing a cleaned reference panel with its neighbour.

8.3. The panels are washed every fifteen days from October to June, and after any dust storm, using demineralised water and soft brushes only; detergent and pressure washers are never used. In the monsoon months the rain does most of the work and washing is monthly. A soiling estimate above three per cent brings the next wash forward.

8.4. The plant has four inverter units. Each is checked once a month for string currents and alarm history, and its log is downloaded to the engineering share; an inverter fault is reported to the solar contractor the same day.

8.5. Nobody walks on the panels; the roof walkways are the only way across, and the roof is closed in rain and wind.

## 9. What the condensers drink

9.1. The site has no municipal supply. Drinking water comes in by tanker to a separate overhead tank with its own filter and is never mixed with the plant supply.

9.2. Water for the evaporative condensers on the plant room roof comes from the borewell at the south-east corner of the plot. Hard water scales a condenser within weeks, so everything drawn from the borewell passes through the softening plant before it reaches the roof.

9.3. Softened water is tested every week by the plant operator, and the reading must stay below 500 ppm; above that, the bleed rate on the condensers is increased the same day and the softener is regenerated.

9.4. Until the second softener was commissioned in May 2026 the limit was looser and the test was monthly; that rule has gone.

9.5. Every weekly reading goes into the water log with the hand-held meter's serial number beside it, and the tds meter itself is checked against a standard solution each month.

9.6. Whatever the tds, condenser sumps are drained and cleaned every three months, and the water-treatment contractor doses biocide under a written chart kept in the plant room.

## 10. Lighting, heating and the office

10.1. All lighting on the site is LED. Chilled and frozen rooms use fittings rated for the temperature, and their drivers sit outside the rooms so that a failed driver can be changed without a person working in the cold.

10.2. Yard lighting is on a photocell with a timer override, and the gate and the dock aprons stay lit all night whatever the timer says. A failed yard light is reported at the shift handover and repaired within two days.

10.3. The office and canteen are heated in winter by heat pumps. Electric bar heaters and fan heaters are not used anywhere on site; a heater found in an office is removed and the person who brought it is told why.

10.4. Floor heating under the frozen block is electric, with its own thermostat and alarm. Its current is read at every monthly energy review, because a heater mat that has failed shows up on the meter long before the floor heaves.

10.5. Office equipment is switched off at the wall at the end of the day except the servers and the alarm panel. The last person to leave the office checks the switches and signs the closing sheet at the gate.

## 11. When the grid is told to give way

11.1. In the paddy season and in very cold spells the utility may order large consumers to reduce their load for set hours. Such a curtailment order arrives by e-mail and by phone to the site manager, who tells the engineering lead and the shift lead at once.

11.2. The site's sanctioned load is 1,450 kW against a contract demand of 1,200 kVA. During an order, loads are switched off in this sequence until the site is under the figure the order gives: first the office and canteen air conditioning, then the yard and apron lighting except the gate, then the softening plant, and only last the ambient store's ventilation. Refrigeration, the frozen block and the controls are never shed.

11.3. If shedding all of those still leaves the site above the figure, the generators take the essential load and the site draws only what the order allows from the grid.

11.4. Each curtailment is logged with the order number, the hours, the loads shed and the diesel used, and the sanctioned figures are reviewed with the utility once a year in the light of that log.

## 12. Records and review

12.1. The substation folder, the switch room log, the generator log, the water log, the energy review notes and the load-shedding log are kept for five years. Laboratory reports on oil and water are kept for the life of the equipment.

12.2. Records are made at the time, in ink or in the building management system, and corrected only by striking through and initialling.

12.3. The engineering lead reviews this manual every August, after the first full summer of each year's figures, and whenever equipment covered by it is added or replaced. A revised manual is issued with a one-page note of what changed and why.

12.4. Suggestions for the manual come to the engineering office from anyone on site, on paper or by e-mail. Each one gets an answer within a month, whether or not it is taken up.

## 13. Electrical work by outside firms

13.1. Outside electricians work on the site only through a work order raised by the engineering office and a permit issued by the shift lead. The permit names the circuit, the isolation point and the person who holds the padlock key, and it is closed only when that person has removed the padlock and signed.

13.2. Every isolation uses a personal padlock and a danger tag. A circuit with two people working on it carries two padlocks, and it is restored only when both have been removed by their owners. Nobody cuts another person's padlock; a padlock whose owner has gone home is removed only by the electrical supervisor after phoning the owner and writing the call in the switch room log.

13.3. Work on the high-tension side of the substation is done only by the utility or by a contractor licensed for high-tension work, with the utility's written shutdown in hand. Our own staff do not operate high-tension switchgear under any circumstances, even to restore supply faster after a fault.

13.4. Cable trenches are opened only after the buried services drawing has been checked and the route marked with paint. The drawing is updated after every trench is closed, and the contractor signs the updated copy.

13.5. Thermal imaging of the main switchboard, the generator panels and the busbar joints is done once a year by a specialist firm, with the site on full summer load. Hot spots are graded in the firm's report, and anything graded urgent is repaired within a fortnight.

13.6. Earthing pits are tested every year before the monsoon. A pit whose resistance has risen above the figure on its tag is watered and retested, and replaced if the retest fails.

13.7. After any electrical work, the contractor's engineer and our electrical supervisor walk the job together before the permit is closed. Tools, offcuts and packaging leave with the contractor; nothing is left in a panel or on a cable tray for someone else to find.
