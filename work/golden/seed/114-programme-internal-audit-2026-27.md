# Internal Audit Programme 2026-27

## 1. Why there is an internal audit team

1.1. Until 2025 Quillfern had no internal audit function of its own. The statutory auditors came once a year, customers audited the sites that held their stock, and certification bodies looked at the quality system, but nobody inside the company was paid to ask, across all departments, whether the controls people relied on were actually working.

1.2. The board set up the Internal Audit team in August 2025, reporting to the board on audit matters and to the Chief Financial Officer for day-to-day administration. This programme is the team's plan for the year from April 2026 to March 2027 and the rulebook it works to.

1.3. The programme is written for three readers. The board needs to know what will be looked at and why. Department heads need to know what will be asked of their teams and when. And the auditors themselves need one place where the working rules are written down, so that two auditors on the same kind of job reach the same kind of conclusion.

1.4. Internal Audit does not run any process, approve any transaction or sign any document on behalf of another department. An auditor who is asked to "just sign this while you're here" declines, however small the item. The team's value lies in having no part in what it examines.

1.5. Nothing in this programme changes an operating procedure. Where an engagement finds that a procedure is unclear or unworkable, the report says so and the procedure's owner decides what to change.

## 2. The team

2.1. The team is three people. Malavika Rane, a chartered accountant who spent eleven years in a firm of auditors before joining, heads it. Ishaan Kale covers finance and procurement engagements. Pooja Natu, who came from a pharmaceutical quality background, covers operations and the sites.

2.2. Engagements that need knowledge the team lacks, such as refrigeration plant or information security, borrow an expert from inside the company for the days needed, or hire one from outside with the board's agreement. A borrowed expert works under the engagement lead's direction and signs the working papers they contribute to.

2.3. The team sits at head office but spends roughly half its working days at the sites. Travel is booked through the normal travel desk and charged to the Internal Audit cost centre, never to the site being audited.

2.4. Each auditor completes at least forty hours of training a year. The record is kept by the head of the team and shown to the board in the annual report.

## 3. How the year's work is chosen

3.1. The plan starts from a risk register built by the team in February each year from interviews with every department head, the previous year's findings, customer audit reports, insurance claims, and anything that has gone wrong badly enough to reach the board.

3.2. Each area on the register is scored for the size of the loss it could cause and for how likely the controls are to fail, and areas are then ranked. The plan covers every area in the top third each year, the middle third every two years and the bottom third at least every three years.

3.3. For 2026-27 the plan holds fourteen engagements. They include the electricity and diesel spend at Ludhiana DC; credit notes raised for returned stock at the Bhosari hub; billing on the Thali Box meal-kit account; service credits under the Vetrix Animal Health contract; payroll and overtime; fuel cards; capital spending on new sites; access to the finance system; and four site walkthroughs chosen late in the year.

3.4. Two weeks of the year are kept free for work the board asks for at short notice. If they are not used by January, they are given to the site walkthroughs.

3.5. The plan is approved by the board in April. A change of more than two engagements during the year goes back to the board; smaller changes are reported at the next board meeting.

## 4. Starting an engagement

4.1. Each engagement begins with a planning memo, written by the engagement lead and agreed by the head of the team. The memo sets out the scope, the period covered, the controls to be tested, the people to be interviewed and the days allowed.

4.2. The department head receives a notification letter at least ten working days before fieldwork begins, with the memo's scope and a list of documents to be ready. Surprise work, such as a cash count, is the only exception, and it is listed in the memo even though the department is not told.

4.3. An opening meeting is held on the first day of fieldwork. The department head or a named deputy attends, and so does anyone whose work will be tested. The meeting goes through the scope and agrees who will be the contact for document requests.

4.4. Requests for documents are made in writing through the agreed contact and logged by the auditor. A request still unanswered after five working days is raised with the department head; after ten, with the head of the team.

## 5. Picking what to look at

5.1. Most controls are tested by picking items from the period under review and checking that the control worked on each one. The number of items depends on how often the control operates, not on the size of the population.

5.2. For a control that operates monthly, attribute testing uses 25 items under random sampling; for one that operates daily or with every transaction, 40 items; for a quarterly control, all of the quarters in the period. A single exception in the items picked is reported, whatever its value.

5.3. For sampling, items are picked at random from a complete listing that the auditor has checked for completeness against a control total. Picking from a listing someone else filtered is not random, however it was produced.

5.4. The 2025 charter used smaller attribute samples; they were raised for this programme because two findings in the first year rested on too few items to persuade anyone. The method and the seed used for random selection are recorded in the working papers so that another auditor could pick the same items.

## 6. How findings are graded

6.1. Every finding is graded high, medium or low by the engagement lead and confirmed by the head of the team. High means a control has failed in a way that could cause a material loss, a breach of law or a harm to a customer's product; low means an improvement that would be nice to have.

6.2. A finding of high severity goes to the auditee's department head in a draft report. The auditee's written response, with an action, an owner and a date, is due within 10 working days. Medium findings are due within 20 working days and low within 30.

6.3. An agreed action on a high finding is due for completion within 90 days of the final report, unless the board agrees a longer date.

6.4. The grade is the auditor's decision. A department may argue with it in its response, and the argument is printed in the final report beside the grade, but the grade is not lowered to get a response signed.

6.5. A finding of medium severity repeated from the previous audit of the same area is raised to high.

## 7. Working papers

7.1. Everything the auditor relies on goes into the working papers: the planning memo, the listings, the items picked, copies of the evidence examined, notes of interviews and the reasons for each conclusion. A conclusion that cannot be traced to a paper in the file is not reported.

7.2. Working papers are kept in the team's restricted folder, organised by engagement number. Paper evidence is scanned on the day it is collected and the paper returned to its owner.

7.3. The head of the team reviews the working papers of every engagement before the draft report is issued, and signs the review sheet. Review comments are cleared in writing, not in conversation.

7.4. Interview notes record what was said, by whom and when, and are sent to the person interviewed for correction within two working days. A person who disagrees with the notes may add their own version to the file.

7.5. Working papers are kept for eight years. They are shown to the statutory auditors on request, and to nobody else without the head of the team's approval.

## 8. Keeping the auditors clean

8.1. An auditor does not audit their own past work, because independence is the whole value of the team. Nobody is placed on an engagement covering a function they worked in, or a decision they took part in, during the previous 24 months. The head of the team checks this when staffing each engagement and records the check in the planning memo.

8.2. Familiarity creeps in slowly, so rotation of leads is planned. An engagement lead takes the same site or department for at most three consecutive audits, after which another auditor leads it; each rotation is fixed a year ahead.

8.3. An auditor who has a family or financial interest in a supplier, a customer or a member of staff whose work is being examined declares it before fieldwork begins. A declared interest usually means a change of staffing, and it never means silence.

8.4. The head of the team reports any threat to the team's independence directly to the board chair, without going through management.

## 9. Counting the money

9.1. Every Quillfern site holds a petty cash float for small purchases that cannot wait for a purchase order: courier charges, a replacement padlock, tea for a visiting inspector. It is an imprest: topped back up to a fixed figure against vouchers, never increased because a month was busy.

9.2. The float is Rs. 15,000 at each site and Rs. 25,000 at head office. Internal Audit counts it by surprise twice a year at every site, with the custodian present, and reconciles cash plus vouchers to the fixed figure.

9.3. A petty cash shortage of more than Rs. 500 is reported to the head of Finance the same day, with the custodian's written explanation.

9.4. The counts in 2025 were yearly and each imprest smaller; both changed in this programme.

## 10. Looking at whole populations

10.1. Some tests are run on every transaction rather than a sample, using data extracted from the finance system. Extracts are taken by the team with read-only access and are never edited; any cleaning is done on a copy and recorded.

10.2. The duplicate payment test runs every month, on the fifth working day, on all supplier payments of the previous month. It looks for the same supplier, amount and invoice number, and for near-matches where one character differs. Every hit is followed up with accounts payable within a week.

10.3. A Benford first-digit test runs once a quarter on staff expense claims, by site and by person. A pattern that departs from the Benford curve is a reason to look, never by itself a finding.

10.4. Results of every run are kept, including runs that found nothing, so that a duplicate missed later can be traced to the run that should have caught it.

## 11. Following up actions

11.1. Every agreed action from every report goes into the actions register, with its owner, its due date and its finding's grade. The register is updated by the team on the first working day of each month from the evidence owners send in.

11.2. An action is closed only on evidence. An e-mail saying "done" is not evidence; a revised procedure, a changed system setting, a signed balance check or a trained-staff list is.

11.3. Overdue actions on high findings are listed by name in each quarterly report to the board, together with the owner's explanation. Overdue actions on medium findings are listed by count.

11.4. Each high finding is tested again by the team about six months after its action is closed, to confirm the fix has held. A fix that has not held reopens the finding at its original grade.

11.5. Owners may ask for one extension of an action date, in writing, before it falls due. A second extension needs the board's agreement.

## 12. Closing an engagement and reporting

12.1. Fieldwork ends with an exit meeting at which the engagement lead goes through every finding with the department head, before anything is written down as final. Nothing should appear in a final report that the department hears of for the first time on paper.

12.2. The final report is issued within 15 working days of that meeting, to the department head, the Chief Financial Officer and the Managing Director, and to the chair of the board's audit committee, which reads every final report at its next quarterly sitting.

12.3. Until this programme the report was allowed 30 working days, and too many reports arrived after the people involved had forgotten the details.

12.4. The committee may call any department head to explain an overdue action in person, and may ask why any report missed its date after the exit meeting.

## 13. Concerns raised in confidence

13.1. Staff, customers and suppliers may raise a concern about fraud, bribery or serious misconduct in confidence, by e-mail to the team's confidential mailbox or by letter to the head of the team at head office. The mailbox is read only by the head of the team.

13.2. Every concern is acknowledged within three working days where the sender has given a way to reply. Anonymous concerns are looked into as far as the information allows; they are never ignored because the sender cannot be asked questions.

13.3. A person who raises a concern in good faith is not disciplined, moved or disadvantaged for it, even if the concern turns out to be mistaken. A manager who tries to find out who raised a concern is reported to the board chair.

13.4. Investigations of concerns are separate from the year's plan. They are run by the head of the team with HR and, where needed, outside lawyers, and are reported to the board chair rather than through the normal report route.

## 14. The team's own quality

14.1. The team reviews its own work after each engagement: what took longer than planned, where evidence was hard to get, and whether the report's findings were accepted. The notes feed the next year's planning.

14.2. Once every three years an outside firm reviews the team against recognised internal audit standards. The first review is planned for 2028.

14.3. Department heads are sent a short questionnaire after each engagement, asking whether the auditors were clear, fair and courteous. The answers go to the head of the team and, in summary, to the board.

14.4. The head of the team reports to the board once a year on the team's performance: engagements completed against plan, findings by grade, actions closed and overdue, and the results of the questionnaires.

## 15. Review of this programme

15.1. The programme is reviewed every March, before the next year's plan is drafted, and revised when the board changes the team's remit or the risk register changes materially.

15.2. Department heads may comment on the draft plan before it goes to the board. Their comments are attached to the plan the board approves.

## 16. Site walkthroughs

16.1. Four engagements each year are short walkthroughs of a site, chosen late so that the site cannot prepare for them specially. A walkthrough takes two days on site and looks at whatever the plan's risk register says is weakest there, together with a fixed core list.

16.2. The fixed core list covers the gate register against the dispatch notes for one week; the issue of numbered stock such as seals and forms; access to the site's offices and records outside working hours; the key register; the fire equipment service tags; and the training files of five people picked from the shift rosters.

16.3. The walkthrough ends with a short written note, not a full report, unless something is found that needs a high grade. The note goes to the site manager and to the operations head within a week.

16.4. Walkthroughs are not inspections of product quality or temperature records. If an auditor sees something that looks like a quality problem, the auditor tells the site's QA lead on the day, writes it in the note and leaves the quality judgement to QA.

16.5. The auditor on a walkthrough wears the site's visitor badge and follows every site rule, including the rules on cold rooms and protective clothing, and is escorted where the site's rules require an escort for visitors.

## 17. Working with the other auditors

17.1. The statutory auditors may rely on Internal Audit's work where they judge it reliable. The head of the team meets the statutory audit partner twice a year, shares the plan in April and the year's reports in January, and gives them access to working papers on request.

17.2. Customer auditors and certification bodies are not given Internal Audit reports. If a customer asks whether Quillfern has an internal audit function, the account manager may say that it does and what it covers in general terms.

17.3. Where a customer's audit finding overlaps with an engagement in the plan, the engagement lead reads the customer's report and its agreed actions before fieldwork, so that the department is not asked the same questions twice and the two sets of actions do not contradict each other.

17.4. Findings from the insurers' surveys and from regulators' inspections are added to the risk register when the team plans the next year. They are not repeated as Internal Audit findings unless the team has tested the matter itself.

17.5. Nothing Internal Audit learns from another auditor's work is passed on outside the company without the head of the team's approval and, where the work belongs to someone else, that party's agreement.

## 18. Records of the team

18.1. The team keeps its plan, its engagement files, the actions register, the confidential concerns log and its reports to the board in its restricted folder. Access is limited to the three members of the team; the board chair may ask for any file.

18.2. The concerns log is kept separately from engagement files and is encrypted. Entries name the concern, the date, the steps taken and the outcome, and they never name the person who raised it unless that person agreed in writing.

18.3. Engagement files and the actions register are kept for eight years. The concerns log is kept for ten years from the closure of each matter.
