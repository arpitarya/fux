# Cold Room Commissioning Manual

## 1. Why commissioning gets its own manual

1.1. A cold room is not finished when the builders leave. It is finished when it has been shown, by tests written down in advance and run by people who did not build it, that it will hold product inside its range on the worst day of the year. Everything between the last panel going up and the first pallet going in is commissioning, and this manual says how Quillfern does it.

1.2. The manual was first written for the opening of Ludhiana DC in 2025 and is now used for the second frozen block at Ludhiana, due for handover in the winter of 2026, and for every new chamber the engineering team builds after it. It applies whether the chamber is built by a turnkey contractor or assembled by our own team from bought-in panels and plant.

1.3. It is written for the engineering lead who runs commissioning, the contractors who build and start the plant, the site QA lead who must accept the room, and the site manager who wants to know when the room can earn money. It does not replace temperature mapping, which follows the company mapping method once commissioning is complete.

1.4. Revision 2 replaces the handover note written in a hurry at the end of the first Ludhiana build. Several of that note's figures were the contractor's defaults, accepted without question; this revision sets Quillfern's own.

## 2. Who does what

2.1. The engineering lead, Gurmeet Kaur Sidhu, owns commissioning and decides the order of tests. Navdeep Grewal, the refrigeration engineer, runs the plant tests with the contractor. The site QA lead accepts or rejects the room for product. The site manager is told the dates and is not asked to approve technical results.

2.2. The refrigeration contractor builds and starts the plant and puts it right when a test fails. The contractor does not decide whether its own work has passed; it provides the readings and the engineering lead decides.

2.3. The panel contractor erects the insulated envelope, the doors and the floor finish. The electrical contractor installs the power, controls and alarms. Each contractor has a named site engineer who attends the tests that concern their work.

2.4. Disagreements between contractors about whose work caused a failure are settled by the engineering lead after the test has been repeated. Nobody argues over a failed reading until it has been confirmed.

## 3. Documents before anything starts

3.1. Before the first test is run, the engineering lead issues a commissioning plan listing every test, its acceptance criteria, the readings to be taken, the instruments to be used and their calibration certificates, and the people who must attend.

3.2. Acceptance criteria are written before the test, not after it. A criterion changed once readings are known is a failure to be explained, not a new criterion.

3.3. The plan is sent to every contractor at least two weeks before the first test. A contractor who disagrees with a criterion says so in writing before testing begins; afterwards it is too late.

3.4. Every instrument used to take a reading has a current calibration certificate traceable to a recognised laboratory. The certificate number is written beside each set of readings on the test sheet.

3.5. Drawings of the room, the plant and the controls are issued in their as-built state before acceptance. A room whose drawings still show the design rather than what was built is not accepted.

## 4. Proving the pipework is tight

4.1. Refrigerant pipework is tested for tightness before any insulation is applied over it and before any refrigerant is admitted. A leak found after the pipes are lagged costs days; one found before costs minutes.

4.2. Every brazed joint is inspected by eye and then the whole system is charged with dry nitrogen at one and a half times its design pressure, isolated from the cylinder and left. The pressure must hold for 24 hours with no fall beyond what the change in ambient temperature explains.

4.3. The first Ludhiana build was held for half that time, and a slow leak at a valve body showed itself only after the room was in service.

4.4. A fall that cannot be explained is searched for with soap solution, joint by joint, and the whole test repeated after repair, from the start.

4.5. The test pressure, ambient temperature, gauge serial and times are recorded at the start, after one hour, and at the end. The nitrogen is vented outdoors and never into a closed room; every brazed repair is followed by a fresh test.

## 5. Taking the air out

5.1. Air and moisture left in a refrigeration system turn into acid and sludge that shorten the life of every component. Before charging, the system is put under vacuum with a pump connected at both the high and low sides.

5.2. The system is pumped down to 500 micron. The pump is then isolated and the system left; it passes if the gauge stays below 1,000 for 30 minutes. A rise above that means moisture or a leak, and the pumping continues or the leak is found.

5.3. The 2025 handover note took the contractor's default figures, which were looser on both counts.

5.4. The gauge used is electronic, connected as close to the system as the hose allows, and never read through the pump's own dial. The readings at isolation, at 10 minutes and at 30 minutes go on the test sheet.

5.5. Pumping is never stopped early because the contractor's crew is due elsewhere. A rushed vacuum is the commonest fault the engineering team finds in other people's plant, and the micron figure is the one most often written down without being read.

## 6. Charging and first start

6.1. Refrigerant is charged by weight from the contractor's scale, with the cylinder number and the weight before and after recorded on the test sheet. The charge must match the designer's figure within the tolerance the designer gives.

6.2. The plant is started with the room empty and its doors shut. The contractor's engineer checks motor currents, oil levels, superheat and the operation of every safety cut-out before the plant is left to run.

6.3. Every safety cut-out is tested by causing the condition it protects against, not by reading its setting. A high-pressure switch is tested by blocking the condenser airflow until it trips, with the engineer standing at the isolator.

6.4. The alarm panel is tested at this stage too: each probe's high and low alarms, door alarms, plant fault alarms, and the call-out to the duty phone. A call that reaches nobody is a failed test.

6.5. Defrost is run manually through at least two full cycles and its termination checked.

## 7. Bringing the temperature down

7.1. A new frozen chamber cannot be cooled as fast as the plant would allow. Water left in new concrete and in the insulation joints freezes, expands and lifts the floor if the room is cooled too quickly, and repairing a heaved floor means emptying the room for months.

7.2. A new chilled chamber is brought straight to its set point. A new freezer is first held at +2 C for 24 hours to let the floor and panels settle, and then brought below 0 C at no more than 2 C per hour until it reaches its set point.

7.3. The controller is programmed with this ramp before the plant starts, and the programme is printed and signed.

7.4. The first Ludhiana freezer was allowed twice that rate; its floor showed hairline cracks at two expansion joints within a year.

7.5. The floor heater mat is proven working before the pulldown begins, and the room temperature is logged every hour during the pulldown, so that the gradient actually achieved can be compared with the programmed one. Any hour faster than the programme stops the process, and any gradient steeper than planned is investigated before cooling resumes.

## 8. Finding where warmth gets in

8.1. When the room has held its set point for 48 hours, the envelope is surveyed from outside for places where cold escapes: panel joints, door frames, pipe and cable penetrations, and the junction between wall panels and floor.

8.2. The survey is done by thermography, with an infrared camera, at night or on a dull day when the outside walls are not heated by the sun. Any joint that shows more than 3 C colder than the panel face beside it is marked, resealed and surveyed again.

8.3. The handover note of 2025 allowed a larger difference before resealing, and two joints it allowed are now visibly iced.

8.4. The images are saved with the room number, the date and the camera's serial number, and printed on the commissioning record with each marked joint circled.

8.5. A second thermography survey with the same infrared camera is done six months after handover, in the opposite season, and its images are compared with the first.

## 9. The envelope, doors and floor

9.1. Every door is tested for its seal by closing it on a sheet of paper at several points around the frame; the paper must not slide out easily. Rapid-roll doors are timed from fully open to fully closed, and their safety edges tested by obstruction.

9.2. Door heaters and frame heaters on freezer doors are checked for current and for warmth by hand at the frame.

9.3. Strip curtains are fitted after the doors are tested, never before, so that the door seal test is not confused by the curtain.

9.4. The floor finish is inspected for cracks and for ponding with water before the room is cooled. Ponding is ground out and refinished; it is not left to freeze.

9.5. Pressure-relief ports in freezer walls are checked for free movement, because a freezer without working relief can buckle its own panels when the door is shut on warm air.

## 10. Racking and the empty room

10.1. Racking is erected after the tightness and evacuation tests and before the temperature tests, so that the room is tested in the form in which it will be used. Racking erected later means testing again.

10.2. Racking is inspected and load-signed by the racking supplier, who fixes load notices to the end of every run. Nobody places a pallet on racking that does not carry its notice.

10.3. Evaporator air paths are checked against the racking layout. A rack run that blocks an evaporator's throw is moved before the room is accepted.

10.4. Sprinklers and smoke detection, where the room has them, are tested by the fire contractor and signed off separately. The commissioning record notes the fire contractor's certificate number but does not repeat its contents.

## 11. Closing out the contractor's list

11.1. As the tests proceed, the engineering lead keeps a single snag list of everything not yet right. Each item is graded A, meaning the room cannot hold product safely until it is fixed, or B, meaning the room can be used while it is fixed.

11.2. Practical completion is certified only when no A item remains open. Every B item must be closed within 30 days of that certificate, and the retention money held from the contractor is not released until the last one is.

11.3. The first Ludhiana build allowed longer, and three of its B items were still open when the retention was paid.

11.4. Each item carries its owner, the dates raised and closed, and the initials of the person who checked the closure. Items are closed on inspection, never on the contractor's word; the punch list is a record of what was seen, not of what was promised.

11.5. A B item still open on day 30 becomes an A item for the purpose of releasing money, though the room stays in use. Contractors who want their retention sooner close their snag items sooner, and argue with the punch grades in writing.

## 12. Signing the room into service

12.1. Commissioning ends with a single signed record that gathers every test sheet, every calibration certificate, the as-built drawings, the closed list and the fire contractor's certificate.

12.2. Every test on the record must have been witnessed, and the record carries two signatories for each: the site QA lead and the refrigeration contractor's engineer. The customer whose stock the room will hold may send an observer, whose name is recorded, but the observer does not sign.

12.3. The 2025 note asked for one signature, the contractor's, and nobody from Quillfern could later say who had watched the pressure test.

12.4. A test that was not witnessed by both signatories is void, and is repeated. Neither signature may be added later by phone or by e-mail.

12.5. Once the record is complete the room passes to the site QA lead for temperature mapping.

## 13. After handover

13.1. The contractor's defects liability period runs for twelve months from practical completion. Faults found in that period are reported to the contractor in writing by the engineering lead, and the contractor's response time is the one written in its contract.

13.2. The plant is serviced in its first year by the contractor who built it, under the contract, and from the second year by the company's approved service partner for refrigeration, unless the two are the same firm.

13.3. The engineering lead reviews the room's alarm history, energy use and defrost behaviour at three months and at twelve months after handover, and writes a short note of anything that needs changing.

13.4. A room that has been altered after handover, by moving racking, replacing an evaporator or changing doors, is treated as a new room for the tests that the change could affect.

## 14. Records

14.1. The signed commissioning record, the test sheets and the thermal images are kept for the life of the room and three years after it is demolished or converted.

14.2. Copies go to the site QA lead, who keeps them with the room's mapping studies, and to the site manager, who keeps them with the lease or the title documents.

14.3. Nothing in the record is rewritten after signing. A correction is a new sheet that refers to the old one and is signed in the same way.

## 15. Review of this manual

15.1. The engineering lead reviews this manual after every new room is handed over, and at least once a year.

15.2. Contractors may suggest changes in writing; each suggestion gets a written answer.

## 16. Safety while the room is being commissioned

16.1. A room under commissioning is a building site, not a cold store. The engineering lead issues a site-specific safety plan before the first test, covering who may enter, what protective equipment is worn, which permits apply and where the nearest first-aid point and assembly point are.

16.2. Nobody works inside a chamber that is below 0 C without a second person outside the door who knows they are there. The door's inside release is tested on the first day the room is cooled, and again on every day the room is entered, by someone opening it from inside with the room lit.

16.3. Work at height on evaporators and on the room's ceiling is done from mobile platforms with guard rails, by people trained to use them. Ladders are used only for short inspections, never for work that needs two hands.

16.4. Hot work near insulated panels is avoided wherever a cold method exists, because the foam in the panels burns fiercely and gives off toxic smoke. Where hot work cannot be avoided, the permit names the fire watch, the extinguishers and the fire blanket to be used, and the panels within reach are shielded.

16.5. Refrigerant cylinders are stored upright, chained, in the shade and outside the building. Empty and full cylinders are kept apart and labelled.

16.6. Every contractor's worker attends the site induction before their first day of work on the room. A worker who has not been inducted is sent off site, whatever the contractor says about the deadline.

## 17. The weather and the calendar

17.1. Commissioning results depend on the weather, and the Punjab calendar is unkind to new plant. A room tested in December has not been tested against a June afternoon, and a room tested in June has not met the fog that keeps outdoor condensers wet for days in January.

17.2. The engineering lead plans the main temperature tests for the hottest weeks available before handover. Where handover falls in winter, the room is accepted on its winter results but a summer performance check is written into the plan for the following May or June, with the contractor required to attend.

17.3. Condenser capacity is checked against the design ambient temperature, not the ambient on the test day. Where the test day is cooler, the contractor shows by calculation, from measured pressures and currents, that the condensers have the margin the design assumed.

17.4. Monsoon rain on an unfinished roof has damaged insulation on other people's projects. No panel is erected and left uncovered overnight between June and September, and roof penetrations are sealed on the day they are cut.

17.5. Fog in December and January slows deliveries of plant and materials from the south. The commissioning plan allows for it rather than asking contractors to make up the time by working at night in a cold room.

17.6. Festivals and harvest seasons take many workers home for weeks. Contractors' labour plans are read against the calendar when the plan is drawn up, and dates are agreed with that in mind rather than discovered later.

## 18. Costs and the commissioning budget

18.1. Commissioning has its own line in every project budget, separate from construction. It pays for the engineering team's time, the calibration of instruments, the hire of a heat camera where the team's own is in use elsewhere, refrigerant losses during testing, and the electricity used while the room runs empty.

18.2. Repeat tests caused by a contractor's failure are charged to that contractor under its contract. Repeat tests caused by a change Quillfern asked for are charged to the project.

18.3. The engineering lead reports commissioning spend against budget to Finance at the end of each month of the project, and explains any line that has gone over.
