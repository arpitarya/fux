# Refrigeration Technician Development Curriculum

## 1. Why we train our own

1.1. Quillfern's new sites need people who can keep a reefer unit, a walk-in chamber and a standby generator running at three in the morning, and the market does not supply enough of them. Experienced mechanics are hired away by the dairies and the pharma makers as soon as they are any good, and the ones who stay with independent garages rarely know anything about temperature records or why a door left open for ten minutes matters.

1.2. In 2024 the engineering pool therefore started its own apprenticeship, run from a workshop in the Waluj industrial area outside Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar (still Aurangabad on most of the signboards). The workshop trains young people in the trade and in the Quillfern way of working, and then places them at the sites that need them.

1.3. This curriculum describes the programme as it runs from the January 2026 intake: who may join, how long it lasts, what the apprentices learn and how they are assessed, what they are paid, and what they may and may not do on live equipment.

1.4. It is written for apprentices, their trainers and mentors, and the site managers who receive them. An apprentice is given a copy on the first day and is expected to have read it by the end of the first week.

## 2. Who can join

2.1. Applicants hold an ITI certificate in refrigeration and air-conditioning, or in an electrical or mechanical trade with a refrigeration module, and are between 18 and 25 on the day the intake opens.

2.2. Applicants sit a written test in arithmetic and basic electrical theory and a short practical in the workshop: wiring a contactor, reading a pressure gauge, brazing a joint. The practical counts for more than the written test.

2.3. A medical examination checks eyesight, colour vision and hearing. Colour vision matters because wiring and refrigerant cylinders are colour-coded.

2.4. Applicants must hold, or obtain within the first three months, a two-wheeler licence. Many floor jobs are reached by motorcycle.

2.5. Quillfern does not charge any fee for the programme, the tests or the medical. An agent who asks an applicant for money is reported to the engineering pool lead, and the applicant is not penalised for having been asked.

## 3. The calendar

3.1. The apprenticeship runs as one continuous programme with no break between the classroom and the floor. Months one to six are mostly in the workshop; months seven onwards are mostly on site, with a day a week back at Waluj.

3.2. From the January 2026 intake the programme lasts 18 months; the 2024 and 2025 intakes served 24. The shorter programme was possible because the workshop now has its own reefer body, cold room and generator, so that practical work no longer waits for a site to lend equipment.

3.3. Apprentices join in two intakes a year, so that there is always a senior group on site while a junior group is in the workshop. Each intake opens on the first working day of January or July and takes twelve trainees, no more. A thirteenth applicant who passes is offered the next intake rather than squeezed in.

3.4. Public holidays follow the Maharashtra list for apprentices at Waluj and the local list for apprentices placed at a site.

3.5. An apprentice who is ill for more than four weeks in a row may move to the next intake and pick up from the module they left.

## 4. The workshop

4.1. The workshop is a rented unit of about 600 square metres with a classroom for twenty, a bench area, a refrigerant recovery bay, a reefer body mounted on stands and a small walk-in cold room built for training. A diesel generator on a trailer is used for changeover exercises.

4.2. The reefer body and the cold room are fitted with the same controllers and loggers as the sites, so that an apprentice who has learned to read them at Waluj can read them anywhere.

4.3. Refrigerant work is done only in the recovery bay, which has extraction, a leak detector and its own fire extinguishers.

4.4. Tools that are too expensive for a personal kit — vacuum pumps, recovery machines, electronic leak detectors, a brazing set — are kept in the workshop store and signed out by the trainer.

4.5. The workshop is open from 08:00 to 17:00, Monday to Saturday. Apprentices do not stay after 17:00 without a trainer present.

4.6. A small library of manufacturers' manuals, wiring diagrams and service bulletins is kept in the classroom. Apprentices may photograph a page for study, but the manuals themselves do not leave the room.

## 5. Money

5.1. Every apprentice is paid a monthly stipend, not a wage, and it rises once, when the apprentice moves from the workshop to the floor. Pay is credited to the apprentice's own bank account on the last working day of the month.

5.2. The stipend is Rs. 14,000 a month for months one to six and Rs. 16,500 from month seven; the 2025 outline gave a flat Rs. 12,000. The rise recognises that floor months are longer and often away from home.

5.3. Apprentices placed at a site away from Waluj receive a lodging allowance and their travel to the site and back at the start and end of the placement. The allowance is set by the site and paid with the stipend.

5.4. Overtime is not paid to apprentices, because apprentices do not work overtime. An apprentice kept beyond the shift by an emergency is given the time back the next day.

5.5. No apprentice is bonded. Nobody repays anything for leaving the programme early or for taking a job elsewhere after it, and nobody is asked to sign a promise to stay.

## 6. Before the first charge

6.1. No apprentice touches a refrigerant charge without the gas-handling certificate, the "gas ticket" in workshop talk. Recovering, charging, venting and leak-testing all count as touching a charge. Until then apprentices may watch, read gauges and wire controls.

6.2. The certificate is sat at the end of month four, and an apprentice who fails it may resit once, in month five. An apprentice who fails the resit leaves the programme, because there is no job on the floor for someone who may not handle refrigerant.

6.3. The certificate is examined by an outside body recognised for refrigerant handling. Quillfern pays the examination fee for the first sitting and the resit.

6.4. The workshop teaches the recovery of every refrigerant used at the sites, including the natural refrigerants on the newer plant, and the reasons why venting is never acceptable.

6.5. An apprentice who holds the certificate carries the card on every job, and a site may turn away an apprentice who cannot show it.

## 7. Modules

7.1. The programme is divided into twelve modules. The first six are taught mainly in the workshop: safety and tools; electrical basics; the refrigeration cycle; controls and loggers; refrigerant handling; reefer units.

7.2. The second six are taught mainly on site: cold rooms and walk-in chambers; generators and changeover; temperature records and what auditors look for; fault-finding; customer sites and conduct; and a final project.

7.3. Each module has a written syllabus, a trainer and a mentor on site, and a logbook in which the apprentice records the jobs done.

7.4. The final project is a real job at the apprentice's site, chosen by the site's engineering lead: a requalification, a controller change or a recovery-and-recharge. The apprentice plans it, does it under supervision and writes it up.

7.5. Module order may change for an apprentice who is placed early or late, but no module is skipped.

## 8. How marks are given

8.1. Every module ends in a theory paper and a practical task. The practical — a hands-on test on real equipment in the workshop or on site — counts for more, because the trade is learned with the hands.

8.2. A practical passes at 70 per cent and a theory paper at 60 per cent; a failed module is repeated with the next intake. An apprentice may repeat no more than two modules in the whole programme.

8.3. Practical tasks are marked against a written checklist by a trainer who did not teach the module, and the checklist is shown to the apprentice beforehand. Safety items on the checklist are pass-or-fail: an apprentice who skips lockout, for example, fails the task whatever else they did well.

8.4. Marks are given back within a week, with the marker's comments. An apprentice who disputes a mark asks the workshop lead for a second marking.

8.5. The final assessment at the end of the programme is held with an examiner from the apprentice's ITI, and leads to the Quillfern certificate of completion.

## 9. Turning up

9.1. Attendance is taken twice a day, at the workshop gate and after lunch, and on site by the mentor. Missed classes count against the apprentice whatever the reason, except leave approved in advance and documented illness.

9.2. Anyone present for less than 85 per cent of a module's sessions repeats that module, whatever their marks. Good marks do not make up for missed practice, because the practice is the point.

9.3. Lateness of more than fifteen minutes counts as half a session missed.

9.4. An apprentice who is going to be absent phones the trainer or mentor before the session starts. A text message is accepted only when the trainer has replied to it.

9.5. Three unexplained absences in a module lead to a meeting with the workshop lead, and a fourth to a written warning.

## 10. On live units

10.1. On a live unit — a working reefer, a cold room with stock in it, a customer's freezer — an apprentice is never the only pair of hands. Live means anything holding product or able to start without warning, and it is the real kit apprentices are here to learn on.

10.2. One qualified technician supervises at most three apprentices on live equipment. On a pharma chamber the ratio is one to one.

10.3. Night work is part of the job later, not at first. No apprentice is put on a night roster before month nine, even with a supervisor. From month nine an apprentice may be rostered at night with a qualified technician on the same job.

10.4. Apprentices never isolate, lock out or restore power on their own until they have passed the electrical module, and never on a pharma chamber during the programme.

10.5. An apprentice who is asked to do something on a live unit that this curriculum forbids says no, and tells the mentor the same day. Saying no is never held against the apprentice.

## 11. Mentors

11.1. Each apprentice placed at a site has a named mentor, a qualified technician who has volunteered and been trained for the role.

11.2. The mentor signs the logbook each week, meets the apprentice for half an hour each fortnight to go through it, and reports to the workshop once a month.

11.3. Mentors receive an allowance for each apprentice they mentor, paid with their salary.

11.4. A mentor may have no more than two apprentices at a time.

11.5. An apprentice who has a problem with a mentor speaks to the workshop lead, who may change the mentor without asking for a reason.

## 12. Kit

12.1. Each apprentice is issued a personal tool kit in the first week: gauges and hoses, a multimeter, a clamp meter, a set of hand tools, a temperature probe, a torch and a tool bag. The kit is engraved with the apprentice's number.

12.2. Trainees often ask whether they get to keep the tools when they finish. The kit, worth about Rs. 22,000, becomes the apprentice's own property on completion; a leaver before completion hands it back.

12.3. A tool lost or broken through ordinary use is replaced by the workshop. A tool lost through carelessness is replaced once without charge; a second loss is discussed with the workshop lead.

12.4. Safety gear — boots, gloves, glasses, a freezer jacket and a helmet — is issued separately and is replaced whenever it wears out.

12.5. Apprentices do not lend their kit to anyone, including qualified technicians on site.

## 13. Placement

13.1. From month seven apprentices are placed at a site that needs them. Placements are decided by the engineering pool lead with the site managers, and apprentices are asked their preference first.

13.2. An apprentice may be placed at a site far from home. The programme pays for travel and lodging and allows one trip home a month.

13.3. A placement lasts until the end of the programme unless the site closes, the mentor leaves or the apprentice asks to move for a good reason.

13.4. At the end of the programme, Quillfern offers a technician's post to each apprentice who completes, at a site with a vacancy. Taking the offer is not compulsory.

13.5. The site manager writes a short report on each apprentice at the end of the placement, and the apprentice sees it.

## 14. Safety on the floor

14.1. The safety rules of the site always apply. Where a site rule and this curriculum differ, the stricter one wins.

14.2. Apprentices wear their safety gear on every job, including short ones.

14.3. Refrigerant cylinders are carried upright and strapped in vehicles. Apprentices never carry a cylinder in a bus or on a motorcycle.

14.4. Brazing is done only with a permit and a fire watch, at the workshop and on site alike.

14.5. Any injury, however small, is reported to the mentor the same day and written in the site's accident book.

## 15. Records and certificates

15.1. The workshop keeps each apprentice's file: application, tests, medical, attendance, marks, logbooks, certificates, mentor reports and any warnings.

15.2. Files are kept for seven years after the apprentice leaves.

15.3. An apprentice may read their own file at any time on request.

15.4. Certificates are issued in the apprentice's name only, and copies are certified by the workshop lead.

15.5. Quillfern gives a reference to any former apprentice who asks, describing what they learned and how they did.

## 16. Welfare

16.1. Apprentices are young and often away from home for the first time. Each intake has a welfare contact at Waluj, a member of the workshop staff who is not a trainer.

16.2. Apprentices may raise anything with the welfare contact — homesickness, money, a problem at the lodging, a problem with a colleague — in confidence.

16.3. The lodging used at each site is visited by the welfare contact before the first apprentice moves in.

16.4. Bullying, ragging or initiation rituals are not tolerated, on site or at the workshop, and are dealt with under the company's discipline rules.

16.5. Apprentices are paid their stipend during approved sick leave for up to four weeks.

## 17. Changes to this curriculum

17.1. Revision 1 (January 2025) set out the programme for the second intake. Revision 2 (June 2026) shortened the programme, raised the stipend in the floor months and set the ratio on live equipment after the first group completed.

17.2. The engineering pool lead reviews the curriculum each May with the workshop staff and two site managers.

17.3. Apprentices and mentors are asked at the end of each module what should change, and their answers are read at the review.
